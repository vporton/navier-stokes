\section{Introduction}

This is a rectified proof by ChatGPT~\cite{navier-stokes-chat} of the Na\-vi\-er-Sto\-kes Clay Math Millennium Prize Problem~\cite{navier-stokes-clay}, using the approach of generalized limits~\cite{limit} (more general generalized functions than distributions) suggested to the LLM (large  language model) by me. In fact, the ChatGPT's proof didn't settle the energy inequality, but only existence of solutions, despite it claimed~\cite{navier-stokes-chat} a full solution.

In my approach multiplication of every two functions is defined, what allows to write formulas undefined for distributions.

So, I did a part of the work (the theorem that $\lim$ (limit) functional can be linearly extended to the space of all functions and that on the space of extended limits all continuous operations can be extended with preservation of all algebraic identities). And ChatGPT did a part of work (applied known methods with addition of my this theorem).

Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence. The claim is a Millennium Prize Problem solution. The evidence of this ``functional extension'' theorem is an extraordinary evidence, because it is a new very powerful theorem in general topology and its proof is extraordinarily longer than a noob would expect.

I had a little trouble to understand the ChatGPT's proof, because while being an expert in general topology, I am no expert in differential equations. But I was able to write down my more detailed (and in parts more elegant than ChatGPT's) proof.

\section{Problem statement}

The problem statement is wider (``weaker'') than the Clay problem as stated.  It allows arbitrary
$N$ and arbitrary smooth $u_0$ and $f$, without assumptions such as
divergence-free initial data, decay, periodicity, or finite energy.  Smooth
functions on $\mathbb{R}^N$ need not be integrable or have finite energy. So, the Clay's statement follows from it.

We are going to prove existence (in the sense of classical real-number solutions) and smoothness~($C^\infty$) of solutions to Na\-vi\-er-Sto\-kes equations on $\mathbb{R}^N$ ($N\in\mathbb{N}$).
Let speed $u(x,t):\mathbb{R}^N\times[0;\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^N$ and pressure $p(x,t):\mathbb{R}^N\times[0;\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$.
\[
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t u - \nu\Delta u + (u \cdot \nabla) u + \nabla p &= f ;\\
\nabla \cdot u &= 0;\\
u({\cdot},0) &= u_0
\end{aligned}
\right.
\]
for viscosity $\nu>0$, $f(t,x)\in C^\infty$ and initial condition $u_0(x)\in C^\infty$.

\section{The limit extension theorems}

We will consider functions $\mathbb{R}^P\to\mathbb{R}^Q$ or sequences $\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^Q$ for some $P,Q\in\mathbb{N}$. We define \emph{generalized limits} of such functions at points of $\mathbb{R}^P$ (and of sequences at infinity) by the formula~(1) in~\cite[p.~6]{limit}:
\begin{itemize}
\item For the group~$G$ in~\cite{limit} take the group of translations of $\mathbb{R}^P$ (or, alternatively, its isometries). For limits of sequences at infinity take~$G$ to be the identity permutations group.
\item On~$\mathbb{R}^Q$ we will consider the proximity~$\mathrm{X}$ with corresponding nearness relation~$\rsuprel{\mathrm{X}}$:
\[ A\rsuprel{\mathrm{X}}B \eqdef \inf_{x\in A,y\in B}\abs{x-y}=0,\quad A,B\in\mathbb{R}^Q. \]

It's easy to show that for this relation, if considered as a funcoid~\cite{volume-1-edition1}, $\mathrm{X}^{-1}\circ\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{X}$ and consequently formula~(3) in~\cite{limit} by~\cite[proposition~6, p.~6]{limit} is valid.
\item It's obvious that $\mathrm{X}$ (as a funcoid~\cite[chapter~7, p.~144]{volume-1-edition1}) commutes with elements of~$G$.
\item Thus generalized limit is defined properly.
\end{itemize}

The following (till the end of this section) is proved in~\cite{limit}.

The generalized limit is defined for every function at all points (or for all sequences at positive infinity).

In~\cite{limit} the set of all generalized limits over $X$ we denote as $\SNG(X)$. We can assume $X\subseteq\SNG(X)$ (by injectivity of $\tau$~\cite[p.~7]{limit}, using that $\mathbb{R}^Q$ is Hausdorff).

We have all derivatives on the set~$X$ to be always defined taking values in~$\SNG(X)$.

\[
\SUPER(X) \eqdef X\cup\SNG(X)\cup\SNG(\SNG(X))\cup\dots
\]

We have all continuous finitary operations on~$\mathbb{R}^Q$ extendable to
\[ \fSNG({-},\mathbb{R}^Q) \subseteq \SUPER(\mathbb{R}^Q) \]
(my kind of generalized functions) \cite[section 9, p.~8]{limit}. For all such operations, algebraic identities ($f(\dots)=g(\dots)$) on~$\fSNG({-},X)$ are the same as on~$X$~\cite[theorem 6, p.~10]{limit}, particularly generalized limit is a linear extension of the usual limit. Also, $\fSNG({-},\mathbb{R}^Q)$ is closed under differential operators.

\section{Continuous supersingular functions}

Let $X$ be some Lebesgue measurable set and some element $0\in X$ (not necessarily real zero or zero vector).

\begin{defn}
Let $\wedge:\SUPER(\mathbb{R})^2\to\SUPER(\mathbb{R})$ be the
levelwise extension of the continuous operation
\[
  (x,y)\mapsto\min\{x,y\}
\]
provided by the limit extension theorem.  For $a,b\in\SUPER(\mathbb{R})$,
define
\[
  a\leq b \eqdef a\wedge b=a.
\]
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}
The above defined relation is a partial order on~$\SUPER(\mathbb{R})$ and
extends the usual order on~$\mathbb{R}$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The identities
\[
  a\wedge a=a,\qquad
  a\wedge b=b\wedge a,\qquad
  (a\wedge b)\wedge c=a\wedge(b\wedge c)
\]
hold on~$\mathbb{R}$ and therefore hold for their levelwise extensions by the
limit extension theorem.  Thus $a\leq a$.  If $a\leq b$ and $b\leq a$, then
\[
  a=a\wedge b=b\wedge a=b.
\]
If $a\leq b$ and $b\leq c$, then
\[
  a\wedge c=(a\wedge b)\wedge c
  =a\wedge(b\wedge c)=a\wedge b=a,
\]
so $a\leq c$.  Finally, for $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$ the equality
$\min\{a,b\}=a$ is equivalent to the usual inequality $a\leq b$.
\end{proof}

The following theorem allows to extend Lebesgue integrals to the space of generalized limits functions in an easy way:

\begin{thm}
If a set $X$ is Lebesgue measurable and $f\in\fSNG({-},\mathbb{R}^N)$ is continuous, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\setcond{x\in X}{f(x)\ne 0}$ is partitioned into Lebesgue measurable sets (\emph{zones})
  \[ \setcond{Z_k}{k\in\SUPER(\mathbb{R}^N)\setminus\{0\}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}, \]
  where zone
  \[ Z_k=\setcond{x\in X}{\exists a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}:f(x)=ka}. \]
\item Each $Z_k$ is an open set in~$X$.
\item $f(x)=0$ for each $x$ on the boundary $X\cap\partial Z$ of each zone.
\item\label{as-func-mult} $f(x)=k_Z g_Z(x)$ for each $x\in X\cap\overline{Z}$ for some $k_Z\in\SUPER(\mathbb{R}^N)\setminus\{0\}$ and $g_Z:X\cap\overline{Z}\to\mathbb{R}$ for each zone $Z$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
To prove that $f(a)=0$ on boundaries of zones, consider two only possible cases for $a\in X\cap\partial Z_k$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item ``$a\in\overline{Z_k}$ and $\forall\epsilon>0\exists x\in\mathbb{R}:(\abs{a-x}<\epsilon\land f(x)=0)$.'' This is possible only if $f(a)=0$ due to continuity of~$f$.

\item ``$a\in\overline{Z_k}$ and $\forall\epsilon>0\exists x\in\mathbb{R}:(\abs{a-x}<\epsilon\land f(x)=k'g(x))$, where $k'\in\SUPER(\mathbb{R}^N)\setminus\{0\}$ and $g:X\cap\overline{Z'}\to\mathbb{R}$ where zone $Z'\ne Z_k$.''
$f(x)$ belongs to the same zone $Z_k$, unless $f(a)=0$, because otherwise we would have $kf(x)=k'f(x)$ for $k$ and~$k'$ from different zones $Z_k$ and~$Z'$, respectively.
\end{enumerate}

$Z_k$ doesn't intersect $\partial Z_k$ and is, therefore, open. Obviously zones don't intersect and their union is $\setcond{x\in X}{f(x)\ne 0}$.

Item~\ref{as-func-mult} is obvious.

We have zones (without loss of generality) being open sets (because not containing points from their boundaries) and therefore Lebesgue measurable.
\end{proof}

\begin{defn}
I call the zone with $k\in\mathbb{R}^N\setminus\{0\}$ \emph{the classical zone}.
\end{defn}

\section{Lebesgue integrals}

By a \emph{supersingular function} or a solution in \emph{supersingularities} I mean a function $f\in\fSNG(X,\mathbb{R})$ (where~$X$ is a suitable space), see~\cite{limit}.

\begin{rem}
(Lebesgue) integrals could be instead defined on the space of supersingular functions the same way as Lebesgue integrals on the space of functions with real number values. This requires existence of (possibly infinite) supremum of a set of supersingularities. But for our purposes it is enough to define integrals on the space of continuous generalized limits functions, and this special case is much simpler to prove properties of.
\end{rem}

Define Lebesgue integrals on the space of continuous generalized limits
functions in the usual way for ring-valued functions, but only in the
absolutely convergent case.  Namely, for a measurable set $A$ and a function
$f$ with values in a compatible real-complete $R$-valued normed module, we
write $\int_A f(x)\,dx$ only when the approximating simple-function sums are
absolutely convergent in the norm topology introduced below; in that case the
integral is the norm limit of those sums.  No general existence theorem is
claimed here.  This is the only case used in the subsequent sections.

It is easy to show that this definition agrees with the usual scalar Lebesgue
integral on real-valued functions and is compatible with scalar multiplication
by real numbers whenever both sides are defined.

Below, we will, contrary to the usual notation, denote~$L^1(X)$ the space of functions on~$X$ integrable by the above formula, not all Lebesgue integrable functions.

\section{Generalized ring-valued Banach and Sobolev spaces}

Let
\[
  R\eqdef\SUPER(\mathbb{R})
\]
with the order defined above.  Let $\mathrm{X}_{\SUPER}$ denote the funcoid on
$R$ obtained from the funcoid of supersingularities in~\cite[section 10,
p.~11]{limit}; explicitly, nearness on different singularity levels is
compared after applying enough copies of the embedding~$\tau$ to place both
sets in the same level.  This funcoid induces a topology on $R$: a set
$U\subseteq R$ is open iff, for every $a\in U$,
\[
  U\in\supfun{\mathrm{X}_{\SUPER}}{\uparrow}\{a\}.
\]
All occurrences of ``continuous generalized-valued function'' below mean
continuous with respect to the ordinary topology on the source and this
$\mathrm{X}_{\SUPER}$-induced topology on the target.  Equivalently, for a
funcoid $\mu$ on the source, the corresponding funcoid-continuity condition is
\[
  f\circ\mu\sqsubseteq \mathrm{X}_{\SUPER}\circ f,
\]
as in~\cite[p.~7]{limit}.

The order on $R$ allows one to define norm-like quantities whenever the
required suprema and integrals exist.  Let
\[
  \abs{a}_{\SUPER}\eqdef a\vee(-a),\qquad
  a\vee b\eqdef -((-a)\wedge(-b)).
\]

\begin{defn}
An $R$-valued normed module is an $R$-module $E$ together with a partially
defined map
\[
  \norm{\cdot}_E:E\to R
\]
such that, whenever the displayed expressions are defined,
\[
  \norm{x}_E\geq0,\qquad
  \norm{x}_E=0\Longleftrightarrow x=0,\qquad
  \norm{ax}_E=\abs{a}_{\SUPER}\norm{x}_E,
\]
and
\[
  \norm{x+y}_E\leq\norm{x}_E+\norm{y}_E.
\]
It is called a generalized seminormed module if the implication
$\norm{x}_E=0\Rightarrow x=0$ is omitted.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}
Let $E$ be an $R$-valued normed module.  Its norm topology $\tau_E$ is generated
by the balls
\[
  B_\epsilon(x)\eqdef
  \setcond{y\in E}{\norm{y-x}_E<\epsilon},
  \qquad \epsilon\in\mathbb{R},\ \epsilon>0,
\]
where the real number $\epsilon$ is embedded into $R\subseteq\SUPER(\mathbb{R})$.
Equivalently, $\tau_E$ is the topology induced by the metric-neighborhood
funcoid whose point-neighborhood filter at $x$ is generated by these balls.
For $E=R$ with $\norm{a}_R=\abs{a}_{\SUPER}$, this norm topology is required to
be at least as strong as the $\mathrm{X}_{\SUPER}$-induced topology; in
applications we use only norms satisfying this compatibility condition.  Hence
norm convergence implies supersingular-funcoid convergence, and norm-continuous
maps are continuous for the funcoid topology.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}
Let $E$ be an $R$-valued normed module.  A sequence $(x_n)$ is real-Cauchy if
for every real $\epsilon>0$ there is $N$ such that
\[
  \norm{x_n-x_m}_E\leq\epsilon
  \quad\text{for all }m,n\geq N.
\]
The module $E$ is called real-complete, or a generalized Banach module, if
every real-Cauchy sequence converges to an element of $E$ in the norm topology
$\tau_E$.  This definition uses real $\epsilon$'s because the classical
continuation theorem requires a genuine real topology.  One may also define a
purely $R$-Cauchy notion by allowing generalized positive $\epsilon\in R$, but
that gives a different and stronger structure and is not presently used.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}
Let $E$ be a compatible real-complete $R$-valued normed module.  A series
$\sum_n x_n$ in $E$ is absolutely convergent if
\[
  \sum_n \norm{x_n}_E
\]
converges in the ordered ring $R$ and its partial sums are bounded above by a
real element of $R$.  An $E$-valued integral is absolutely convergent if its
approximating simple-function sums satisfy the analogous condition.  In both
cases convergence of the original series or integral means convergence in the
norm topology~$\tau_E$, and then also convergence in the
$\mathrm{X}_{\SUPER}$-funcoid topology by compatibility.
\end{defn}

\begin{thm}
Let $E$ and $F$ be compatible real-complete $R$-valued normed modules, and let
$T:E\to F$ be a continuous $R$-linear map.  Then $T$ preserves real-Cauchy
sequences and absolutely convergent series.  Moreover, whenever an integral
$\int_A f(x)\,dx$ exists in $E$, the integral $\int_A T(f(x))\,dx$ exists in
$F$ and
\[
  T\left(\int_A f(x)\,dx\right)=\int_A T(f(x))\,dx.
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Continuity gives $x_n\to x$ in $E$ implies $T(x_n)\to T(x)$ in $F$.  Hence a
real-Cauchy sequence or an absolutely convergent series in $E$ is sent to a
real-Cauchy sequence or absolutely convergent series in $F$.  The integral
statement follows by applying the same argument to the defining simple-function
approximations and using linearity of $T$.
\end{proof}

For a generalized-valued scalar or vector function $v$ define, when the
right-hand side exists,
\[
  \abs{v(x)}_{\SUPER}
  \eqdef \left(\sum_i \abs{v_i(x)}_{\SUPER}^2\right)^{1/2}.
\]
For $1\leq p<\infty$ define the ring-valued $L^p$ quantity
\[
  \norm{v}_{L^p_{\SUPER}}
  \eqdef \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}\abs{v(x)}_{\SUPER}^p\,dx\right)^{1/p},
\]
and define
\[
  \norm{v}_{L^\infty_{\SUPER}}
  \eqdef \esssup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^N}\abs{v(x)}_{\SUPER}
\]
when this essential supremum exists in the ordered ring
$R$.

Define $L^p_R(\mathbb{R}^N)$ to be the set of generalized-valued measurable
functions for which $\norm{v}_{L^p_{\SUPER}}$ is defined and finite in $R$.
After quotienting by the relation $\norm{v-w}_{L^p_{\SUPER}}=0$, this is an
$R$-valued seminormed module, and it is an $R$-valued normed module if the
zero-norm quotient is separated.

For an integer $m\geq0$ define the ring-valued Sobolev quantity
\[
  \norm{v}_{H^m_{\SUPER}}^2
  \eqdef \sum_{\abs{\alpha}\leq m}
  \norm{\partial^\alpha v}_{L^2_{\SUPER}}^2,
\]
whenever all derivatives, integrals, sums, and square roots are defined.

Define
\[
  H^m_R(\mathbb{R}^N)
  \eqdef
  \setcond{v\in\fSNG({-},R^N)}
  {\partial^\alpha v\in L^2_R(\mathbb{R}^N)\text{ for every }\abs{\alpha}\leq m}
\]
modulo the zero $H^m_{\SUPER}$ seminorm.  When the quotient is separated and
real-complete, $H^m_R(\mathbb{R}^N)$ is a generalized ring-valued Sobolev
Banach module.  If the inner product
\[
  \langle v,w\rangle_{H^m_R}
  \eqdef
  \sum_{\abs{\alpha}\leq m}
  \int_{\mathbb{R}^N}\partial^\alpha v\cdot\partial^\alpha w\,dx
\]
is defined, it is also a generalized Hilbert module in the formal sense that
$\norm{v}_{H^m_{\SUPER}}^2=\langle v,v\rangle_{H^m_R}$.

Similarly, operator norms can be defined by
\[
  \norm{A}_{X_{\SUPER}\to Y_{\SUPER}}
  \eqdef
  \sup_{\norm{v}_{X_{\SUPER}}\leq 1}\norm{Av}_{Y_{\SUPER}},
\]
when the displayed supremum exists.  These definitions are partial, just as
classical operator norms are meaningful only for operators acting between
specified normed spaces.

\begin{rem}
These are Banach/Sobolev structures in the generalized ring-valued sense above.
They are not automatically classical Banach spaces.  A continuation theorem for
classical Na\-vi\-er-Sto\-kes solutions still needs a real-valued bound, for
instance a real number $M$ such that
\[
  \norm{u(t)}_{H^m_{\SUPER}}\leq M
  \quad\text{for }0<t<T.
\]
When $u(t)$ is classical, this ring-valued Sobolev quantity agrees with the
usual $H^m$ norm, so such a real bound implies the classical bound required in
the endpoint argument.
\end{rem}

\begin{thm}
Let $E$ be a compatible real-complete $R$-valued normed module.  If an
$E$-valued integral is absolutely convergent on a measurable set, then it is
convergent.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
For series, the triangle inequality gives
\[
  \norm{\sum_{k=m}^n x_k}_E
  \leq\sum_{k=m}^n\norm{x_k}_E.
\]
Since the series of norms converges, its tails are smaller than every real
$\epsilon>0$ eventually; hence the partial sums of $\sum_n x_n$ are real-Cauchy
in $E$.  The proof for integrals is the same argument applied to the
approximating simple-function sums.  Real-completeness gives a limit in the
norm topology, and compatibility with $\mathrm{X}_{\SUPER}$ gives convergence
in the supersingular-funcoid topology as well.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Properties of Lebesgue integrals}

\begin{thm}
The fundamental theorem of calculus holds for integrals of continuous functions on the space of generalized limits functions:

Let $a<b$ be real numbers. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $f\in L^1([a,b])$ (absolutely integrable by Lebesgue) and
\[ F(x) = \int_a^x f(t) dt \quad\text{for } x\in [a,b]\]
then $F$ is absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable almost everywhere on $[a,b]$, and $F'(x) = f(x)$ almost everywhere on $[a,b]$.
\item If $G\in\fSNG([a,b],\mathbb{R})$ is absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$, $G'$ exists almost everywhere, and $G'(x) \in L^1([a,b])$, then
\[ G(x)-G(a) = \int_a^x G'(t) dt \quad\text{for } x\in [a,b].\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Let $f\in L^1([a,b])$ and $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t) dt$. Then because each zone (for function~$f$) $Z$ is an open set, it is representable as at most countable union~$I_Z$ of intervals. Moreover, then the entire union of all zones $\bigcup_{Z\in S} I_Z$ is representable as at most countable union~$I'$ of intervals. The interior $\interrior\setcond{x\in [a,b]}{f(x)=0}$ is also representable as at most countable union~$I''$ of intervals. Let denote the set of all such intervals $I = I'\cup I''$. The set of points on the borders of these intervals~$I$ is therefore at most countable and thus negligible.

Let us denote $K=\setcond{J\in I'}{\max J<x}$.

Therefore either $x\in J$ for some~$J\in I'$ or $x\in J$ for some $J\in I''$ ($f(x)=0$). Consider both cases

\begin{itemize}
\item If $x\in J$ for some $J\in I'$ then
\[ F(x) = \int_{\bigcup K} f(t) dt + \int_{J\cap [a,x]} f(t) dt \]
(This integral is absolutely convergent because $K$ is at most countable.)

Obviously, $F$ is absolutely continuous at~$x$, differentiable at~$x$ (except of a negligible set of points) and \[ F'(x) = 0 + \left(x\mapsto\int_{J\cap [a,x]} f(t) dt\right)' = f(x). \]

\item If $x\in J$ for some $J\in I''$, then it's obvious.
\end{itemize}

The other direction:

Let $G\in\fSNG([a,b],\mathbb{R})$ be absolutely continuous on $[a,b]$, $G'$ exists almost everywhere, and $G'(x) \in L^1([a,b])$.

We have for suitable~$k_j$
\begin{multline*}
\int_a^x G'(t) dt =
\sum_{j\in K}\int_j k_j (G'(t)/k_j) dt + \int_{J\cap [a,x]} k_J (G'(t)/k_J) dt =\\
\sum_{J\in K}k_j\int_j (G'(t)/k_j) dt + k_J\int_{J\cap [a,x]} k_J (G'(t)/k_J) dt =\\
\sum_{J\in K}k_j(G(\inf j) - G(\sup j))/k_j + k_J(G(x) - G(a))/k_J =\\
\sum_{J\in K}(G(\inf j) - G(\sup j)) + (G(x) - G(a)) = G(x) - G(a),
\end{multline*}
because $G'$ is zero between zones.
\end{proof}

\section{Inhomogenious linear differential equations}

Theorem elaborated from~\cite{duhamel-chat}.

\begin{defn}
\emph{Inhomogeneous linear evolution} with~$\Delta$ as the differential operator is
\begin{equation*}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
(\partial_t - \Delta)u &= f;\\
u({\cdot},0) &= 0.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation*}
\end{defn}

\begin{thm}[First order Duhamel's principle]
Define auxiliary equations
\begin{equation*}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
(\partial_t - \Delta)u_s &= 0;\\
u_s(x,s) &= f(x,s).
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation*}
If the above family of equations have continuous in~$s$ solutions~$u_s(x,t)$ in supersingularities then the function
\[ u(x,t) = \int_0^t u_s(x,t) ds \]
is a continuous solution of the inhomogeneous linear evolution equation in supersingularities.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Continuity of $u_s$ makes the integral defined.

$u({\cdot},0) = 0$ is obvious.

By the Leibniz rule for an integral with moving upper limit,
\[ \partial_t u(x,t) = \partial_t \int_0^t u_s(x,t) ds = u_t(x,t) + \int_0^t \partial_t u_s(x,t) ds. \]
\begin{multline*}
(\partial_t - \Delta)u(x,t) = \partial_t \int_0^t u_s(x,t) ds - \Delta \int_0^t u_s(x,t) ds =\\
u_t(x,t) + \int_0^t (\partial_t u_s(x,t) - \Delta u_s(x,t)) ds = f(x,t),
\end{multline*}
so $(\partial_t - \Delta)u = f$.
\end{proof}

\section{Rewriting in integral form}

Let denote Leray projection as~$\mathbb{P}$.

\begin{lem}
If $u$ is a $C^\infty$ super-sin\-gu\-lar function, then \[ e^{\nu (t-s) \Delta} \mathbb{P} \nabla \cdot (u \otimes u)(s) \] is a continuous super-sin\-gu\-lar function for $s\in[0,t]$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Obvious.
\end{proof}

\begin{defn}
I call the following defined for supersingular continuous functions:
\[
u(t) = e^{\nu t \Delta} u_0 - \int_0^t e^{\nu (t-s) \Delta} \mathbb{P} \nabla \cdot (u \otimes u)(s) ds
+ \int_0^t e^{\nu (t-s) \Delta} \mathbb{P} f(s) ds
\]
\emph{supersingular mild solutions}\footnote{This formula is usually called \emph{mild (Duhamel) formulation}.} of Na\-vi\-er-Sto\-kes equations.
\end{defn}


\begin{thm}
$C^\infty$ supersingular mild solutions of Na\-vi\-er-Sto\-kes equations are exactly $C^\infty$ solutions of Na\-vi\-er-Sto\-kes equations (without the requirement that~$p$ is $C^\infty$) in supersingularities.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
It is known that \[ \widehat{\mathbb{P}f}(\xi) = \left(I - \frac{\xi \otimes \xi}{|\xi|^2}\right)\hat{f}(\xi). \]

$\mathbb{P}$ is obviously a linear operator.

$\mathbb{P}\nabla q=0$ for any scalar~$q$.

$\mathbb{P}$ commutes with $\Delta$ and with the heat semigroup: 
$\mathbb{P}\Delta=\Delta\mathbb{P}$,
$\mathbb{P}e^{\nu t \Delta}=e^{\nu t \Delta}\mathbb{P}$. (It's clear from these equalities in the classical case and linear combinations for the zones.)

Apply $\mathbb{P}$ to the PDE (partial differential equation). Since $\mathbb{P}\nabla p=0$, we get
\[ \mathbb{P}\partial_t u - \nu\mathbb{P}\Delta u - \mathbb{P}((u\cdot\nabla) u) = \mathbb{P}f. \]

Because $u$ is divergence-free and $\mathbb{P}$ equals identity on divergence-free fields, $\mathbb{P}u=u$. $\mathbb{P}$ commutes with $\partial_t$ and $\Delta$.

Thus we have the consequence
\[ \partial_t u - \nu\Delta u + \mathbb{P}((u\cdot\nabla) u) = \mathbb{P}f. \]

We can write the nonlinear term as a divergence of a tensor:
\[ (u\cdot\nabla) u = \nabla \cdot (u\otimes u), \]
because $(u\otimes u)_{ij}=u_i u_j$ and $\nabla \cdot (u\otimes u) = \sum_j\partial_j (u_i u_j)$.

Thus the PDE becomes: \[ \partial_t u - \nu\Delta u + \mathbb{P}\nabla \cdot (u\otimes u) = \mathbb{P}f. \]

Rewrite this PDE as a system:
\[ \partial_t u - \nu\Delta u = -F(t) + \mathbb{P}f(t),\quad F(t) = \mathbb{P}\nabla \cdot (u\otimes u). \]

Denote $G_t = e^{\nu t\Delta}$.

For the linear homogeneous heat equation $\partial_t v - \nu\Delta v = 0$ with initial data $v(0)=v_0$ the solution is $v(t) = G_t v_0$.

For the inhomogeneous equation, the Duhamel principle (variation-of-constants formula) gives the unique solution as
\[ u(t) = G_t u_0 + \int_0^t G_{t-s} \bigl( -F(s) + \mathbb{P}f(s) \bigr) \, ds = G_t u_0 - \int_0^t G_{t-s} F(s) \, ds + \int_0^t G_{t-s} \mathbb{P}f(s) \, ds. \]

Substituting the value of~$F$ gives exactly the mild formula.

Now prove in the converse: if continuous~$u$ satisfies the mild equation, then it satisfies the PDE and we can recover a pressure~$p$. The below proof is correct only almost everywhere in time. But since the functions in question are~$C^\infty$, it extends to everywhere.

Assume $u$ to be a continuous supersingular function.

Differentiate the mild equation in~$t$. Use facts:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\partial_t(e^{\nu t \Delta}u_0) = \nu \Delta e^{\nu t \Delta} u_0$;
\item $\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^t e^{\nu (t-s) \Delta} F(s) ds = F(t) + \int_0^t \nu e^{\nu (t-s) \Delta} F(s) ds$.
\end{itemize}
Apply this with $F(s) = \mathbb{P}\nabla \cdot (u\otimes u)(s)$. Differentiating mild gives:
\begin{multline*}
\partial_t u(t) = \nu \Delta e^{\nu t\Delta}u_0 - \mathbb{P}\nabla \cdot (u\otimes u)(t) + {} \\ \int_0^t \nu\Delta e^{\nu (t-s)\Delta} \mathbb{P}\nabla \cdot (u\otimes u)(s) ds + \mathbb{P}f(t) - \int_0^t \nu\Delta e^{\nu (t-s)\Delta} \mathbb{P}f(s) ds.
\end{multline*}
Group terms:
\[ \partial_t u(t) - \nu \Delta u(t) = \mathbb{P}\nabla \cdot (u\otimes u)(t) + \mathbb{P}f(t). \]
This is exactly the projected PDE.

Since $\mathbb{P}$ annihilates gradients, applying $(I-\mathbb{P})$ to the PDE recovers the pressure gradient term: start from the original PDE form:
\[ \partial_t u - \nu\Delta u + (u \cdot \nabla) u + \nabla p = f. \]

We already have projection $\mathbb{P}$ of this equation satisfied. To obtain $\nabla p$ explicitly, take divergence of the PDE:
\[ \nabla \cdot \bigl(((u\cdot\nabla)u) + \nabla p - f\bigr) = 0 \]
because
\[ \nabla \cdot (\partial_t u - \nu\Delta u) = \partial_t(\nabla\cdot u) -\nu\Delta(\nabla\cdot u) = 0 \]
when $\nabla\cdot u=0$ initially and evolution preserves it (or check separately).

Use $\nabla\cdot(u\cdot\nabla)u = \sum_{i,j}\partial_i\partial_j(u_i u_j)$. Thus
\[ -\Delta p = \nabla \cdot \nabla (u\otimes u) - \nabla \cdot f\quad\text{(Poisson)}. \]
It is common knowledge that the Poisson equation's solution is $C^\infty$ if $u$ and $f$ are $C^\infty$.

Because $u$ is smooth, we can split this equality into zones, and it is true in every zone.
On borders of zones, it can be constructed as a limit of the Poisson equation's solution in a zone. It does not depend on choice of the zone we take limit in, because~$u\in C^\infty$. Outside of zones it's zero.
So, we have supersingular solution, too.
\end{proof}

\section{The sequence of approximate solutions}

Define the Picard iteration mapping~$\mathcal{T}$ on ti\-me-de\-pen\-dent $C^\infty$ vector fields by
\begin{equation*}
(\mathcal{T}v)(t) = G_t u_0 - \int_0^t G_{t-s}\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(v\otimes v)(s)ds + \int_0^t G_{t-s}\mathbb{P}f(s)ds.
\end{equation*}
So, we have a sequence
\[ u^{(0)}(t) = u_0,\quad u^{(n+1)}(t) = (\mathcal{T}u^{(n)})(t). \]

Note that the integrand is~$L^1$ because $\nabla\cdot(v\otimes v)$ is~$C^\infty$.

Each element of this sequence is a continuous supersingular function.

Try as the solution $u(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}u^{(n)}(t)$ the generalized limit of this sequence.

\section{Interchange of limit and integration}

Let $\lim$ be our extended limit. On each (closed) zone (a closed interval), $\lim$ is a bounded linear operator. Therefore (because it is a linear bounded operator~\cite{int-banach} $\lim$~can be interchanged with integrals.)
It also maps absolutely convergent series to absolutely convergent series, therefore this extends to the entire closed interval. Thus we have:

\begin{multline*}
u(t) = \lim_{n\to\infty}u^{(n)}(t) =\\ G_t u(0) - \int_0^t \lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(G_{t-s} \mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u^{(n)}\otimes u^{(n)})(s)\bigr)ds + \int_0^t G_{t-s} \mathbb{P}f(s)ds =\\ G_t u(0) - \int_0^t G_{t-s} \mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)(s)ds + \int_0^t G_{t-s} \mathbb{P}f(s)ds,
\end{multline*}
that is it is a solution.

\begin{thm}
The heat semigroup $G_t$ is infinitely smoothing, that is $G_t f\in C^\infty$ for any $f\in\fSNG({-},\mathbb{R})\cap L^1$ for $t>0$. (The norm is taken in~$\fSNG({-},\mathbb{R})$.)
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Apply the classical result for each closed zone~$\overline{Z}$ of~$G_t f$. (Outside closed zones, points have a neighborhood where $G_t f$ is zero and thus smooth, too.)
\end{proof}

Therefore the integral~$u$ is $C^\infty$ (on the set $\fSNG({-},\mathbb{R}^N)$).

It remains to prove that $u$ takes on values in~$\mathbb{R}^N$ (is classical).

Rewrite the equation as:

G_t u(0) - u(t) = \int_0^t G_{t-s} \bigl(\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)(s) - \mathbb{P}f(s)\bigr)ds.
\end{equation}

\subsection{A zone-free endpoint criterion}

The zone argument should not be used.  The standard way to prove that a
candidate solution is classical at a possible critical time is instead a
continuation criterion in a classical Banach space.

Let $X$ be a classical solution space for which the local Na\-vi\-er-Sto\-kes
theory is available, for instance $H^m(\mathbb{R}^N)$ with
$m>N/2+1$ and divergence-free data.  Suppose that the generalized mild solution
$u$ agrees with a classical solution on $[0,T)$ and that, for some real
number $M$,
\begin{equation}\label{endpoint-bound}
  \sup_{0<t<T}\norm{u(t)}_X \leq M < \infty .
\end{equation}
Then $u(t)$ has a limit in $X$ as $t\uparrow T$ provided the mild formula gives
the usual time-continuity estimates in $X$.  More explicitly, for
$0<r<t<T$,
\begin{multline*}
u(t)-u(r)
= (G_{t-r}-I)u(r) \\
-\int_r^tG_{t-s}\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)(s)\,ds
-\int_0^r(G_{t-s}-G_{r-s})\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)(s)\,ds \\
{}+\int_r^tG_{t-s}\mathbb{P}f(s)\,ds
{}+\int_0^r(G_{t-s}-G_{r-s})\mathbb{P}f(s)\,ds .
\end{multline*}
If the terms on the right tend to zero in $X$, uniformly for $r,t\uparrow T$,
then $u(t)$ is Cauchy in $X$.  Since $X$ is complete, there is
$u_T\in X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^N$ with $u(t)\to u_T$ in $X$.  Applying the local
existence theorem with initial data $u_T$ continues the classical solution
beyond $T$.

Thus, a zone-free proof of classicality at $t_{\operatorname{crit}}$ would
follow from a classical a priori bound such as~\eqref{endpoint-bound}, together
with the corresponding time-continuity estimates for the nonlinear Duhamel
term.  The present generalized-limit construction does not yet provide this
bound.  Therefore the claim that $u(t_{\operatorname{crit}})$ is classical is
not proved by the current argument.

\begin{rem}
Using the ring-valued Sobolev quantity above, a sufficient form of
the missing bound would be
\begin{equation}\label{super-endpoint-bound}
  \norm{u(t)}_{H^m_{\SUPER}}\leq M
  \quad\text{for all }0<t<T
\end{equation}
with one real number $M$.  Since $u(t)$ is classical on $[0,T)$, the
ring-valued norm agrees there with the ordinary $H^m$ norm, and
\eqref{super-endpoint-bound} implies~\eqref{endpoint-bound}.
\end{rem}

\begin{rem}
The estimate~\eqref{super-endpoint-bound} cannot be obtained merely by
repeating the classical local theory in $\SUPER$ or $\fSNG$.  For
$X=H^m(\mathbb{R}^N)$, $m>N/2+1$, the classical calculation gives a differential
inequality of the form
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}\norm{u(t)}_{H^m}
  \leq C\norm{u(t)}_{H^m}^2+\norm{f(t)}_{H^m},
\]
which gives a bound only on a short time interval depending on the initial
norm.  Iterating this local estimate up to a prescribed time $T$ requires an
independent a priori bound preventing the $H^m$ norm from blowing up before
$T$.  In dimension three, proving such a bound for arbitrary smooth data is
precisely the unresolved regularity problem.

The ring-valued version of the same computation would at best give a
ring-valued differential inequality, assuming all products, integrals, and
suprema involved are defined.  To use it for classical continuation one still
needs a comparison theorem showing that the ring-valued inequality implies a
real bound~\eqref{super-endpoint-bound}.  One also needs a theorem that the
generalized limit of the Picard iterates preserves uniform real $H^m$ bounds:
\[
  \sup_n\norm{u^{(n)}(t)}_{H^m}\leq M
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \norm{\xlim_{n\to\infty}u^{(n)}(t)}_{H^m_{\SUPER}}\leq M .
\]
Without such comparison and boundedness theorems, the ring-valued norms do not
justify~\eqref{endpoint-bound}.
\end{rem}

For further contradiction to come, one would need to prove such an estimate and
then assume that such $t_{\operatorname{crit}}$ exists.

\section{Errors and insufficiently justified steps}

This is the opinion (now edited by me) of Open AI's GPT-5.5 Medium on my proof.

The preceding argument does not prove global existence of classical solutions.
Some points below have been partly addressed by adding definitions or by
replacing an invalid argument with a conditional criterion, but none of the
main analytic gaps is fully resolved.  The status of each issue is recorded
explicitly.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Unresolved.} The zone sets do not form the claimed indexed partition as written.  If
$k'=ck$ for a nonzero real scalar $c$, then $Z_{k'}=Z_k$, so distinct indices
can name the same nonempty set.  The proof that zone boundaries have value zero
also assumes without proof that the only alternative to nearby zero values is a
single fixed neighboring zone.  A neighborhood can meet infinitely many
different zones.  The multiplication argument in that case does not establish
the conclusion.  Consequently openness, measurability, and the representation
on the closure of each zone are not established.

\item \textbf{Resolved at the level of exposition.} The funcoid on
$\SUPER(\mathbb{R})$ from~\cite[section 10, p.~11]{limit} specifies the target
topology for generalized-valued continuity, and the ring-valued norms above
specify compatible norm topologies and real-complete modules for absolute
convergence.  The integral is now treated explicitly as a partial operation:
it is invoked only after the corresponding simple-function sums are assumed or
shown to converge in the compatible complete space where the calculation is
being carried out.  No general existence theorem is claimed.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} The generalized-valued fundamental theorem of calculus is not proved.
The interval decomposition does not cover all relevant points: the complement
of the union of the zones and the interior of the zero set can contain a
positive-measure boundary set.  Local differentiability on component intervals
does not prove global absolute continuity.  In the converse direction the
calculation divides by generalized values without justification, has
inconsistent indices and an extra factor $k_J$, uses reversed endpoint
differences, and assumes a telescoping identity that has not been established.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} The proof of Duhamel's principle assumes precisely the analytic facts that
need proof in the new setting.  Continuity alone does not imply that the
newly-defined integral exists.  The Leibniz rule, differentiation under the
integral sign, and commutation of $\Delta$ with the integral are not justified
for generalized-valued functions.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} The Leray projection and heat semigroup are nonlocal operators.  Their
classical Fourier formulas do not automatically extend by ``linear
combinations for the zones.''  Function spaces on which these operators act,
their mapping properties, and their compatibility with generalized limits must
be proved.  The lemma declaring the nonlinear integrand continuous is therefore
not obvious.  Moreover, the statement that the integrand is $L^1$ because it is
$C^\infty$ is false on $\mathbb{R}^N$.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} There are sign errors in the projected-equation computation.  Applying
$\mathbb{P}$ to the displayed Na\-vi\-er-Sto\-kes equation gives a plus sign in
front of $\mathbb{P}((u\cdot\nabla)u)$, contrary to one displayed line.
Differentiating the mild formula should give
\[
\partial_tu-\nu\Delta u
=-\mathbb{P}\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)+\mathbb{P}f,
\]
not the formula displayed in the converse calculation.  The signs of the
integral terms in that differentiation are also reversed.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} Recovery of the pressure is incomplete.  It requires an appropriate
Helmholtz decomposition and a solution theory for the Poisson equation in
specified function spaces.  The displayed Poisson formula has ambiguous
notation, and smoothness of the data alone does not justify the proposed
zone-by-zone construction or the claim that the constructions agree on zone
boundaries.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} The Picard iteration is not shown to converge in any classical function
space, nor is it shown to remain in a space on which all displayed integrals
exist.  Taking an extended limit of an arbitrary sequence does not replace the
estimates needed for convergence.  It is also necessary to prove shift
invariance
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}u^{(n+1)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}u^{(n)}
\]
before passing from $u^{(n+1)}=\mathcal{T}u^{(n)}$ to a fixed-point equation.

\item \textbf{Partly addressed, but unresolved.} The introduction of
ring-valued Sobolev quantities gives some formal language for norms, but the
interchange of generalized limit and integration is still unsupported.
Linearity of an algebraic extension of the usual limit does not imply
boundedness, and the assertion that the limit is bounded on each closed zone is
not proved in a classical Banach-space topology.  The same problem applies to
moving the limit through multiplication,
differentiation, $\mathbb{P}$, and $G_t$.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} The smoothing theorem is not obtained by applying the classical heat
semigroup theorem separately on zones.  Heat flow is nonlocal and mixes values
from different spatial regions.  In addition, estimates near $s=t$ are needed
to infer smoothness of the time integral in the mild formula.  These estimates
are absent.

\item \textbf{Partly addressed, but unresolved.} The previous zone-based
endpoint argument has been replaced above by a zone-free continuation
criterion, so the specific zone-boundary argument is no longer being relied on.
However, the criterion is conditional: the needed real a priori bound
\eqref{endpoint-bound}, or a comparison theorem implying it from
\eqref{super-endpoint-bound}, has not been proved.  No first nonclassical time
is rigorously constructed, and pointwise real-valuedness is still not enough to
apply a local well-posedness theorem.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} The continuation principle cannot be applied merely because values at
$t_{\operatorname{crit}}$ are claimed to be classical.  One needs control of
an appropriate norm of the solution as $t\uparrow t_{\operatorname{crit}}$.
Obtaining such control is the central unresolved global-regularity issue.  The
existing \verb|\fxwarning{Prove}| at the gluing step records an additional
unproved assertion rather than completing this argument.

\item \textbf{Unresolved.} The energy-inequality section does not fill this gap.  Smoothness alone
does not give a finite-energy inequality for arbitrary smooth data on
$\mathbb{R}^N$, and the standard energy inequality does not provide the
three-dimensional regularity bound needed for global continuation.
\end{enumerate}
